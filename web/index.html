Core Pricing in Learned Assignment Markets

When a ride-hailing platform matches riders to drivers using predicted (noisy) surplus values, how much do the optimal matching and the stable (core) prices change with prediction error, can riders or drivers gain by misreporting, and which stable price should the platform choose?

A predicted surplus is modelled as the true surplus plus Gaussian error. Every number on this page is computed in your browser. Anirudh Jain, Ayush Pandey, Sourabh.

The market and its matching

Each entry is a rider’s value for the trip minus the driver’s cost. The assignment LP picks the matching with the largest total, and the dual LP sets how each matched pair splits its surplus.

Core prices

Any split inside these ranges is stable: no rider and driver can leave together and both do better. Rider-optimal (RO) and driver-optimal (DO) are the two ends, and the midpoint (MID) is their average. The marker shows the price point chosen above.

Prediction error

Each draw adds N(0, σ2) error to every entry, solves the estimated market, and compares it with the true one. The random seed is fixed, so the same settings give the same numbers.

Misreporting

One rider shifts their own row, or one driver their own column, by −2, −1, +1 or +2. The gain is what they really earn under the false report minus what they earn by telling the truth. Select a cell to see who gains.

Which price should the platform choose?

A zero-sum game. The platform picks RO, MID or DO, a manipulator picks which side to act on, and the payoff is the best gain from the table above. The platform wants it small.
